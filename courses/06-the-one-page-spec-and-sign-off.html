The one-page spec, signed and kept signed

Five sessions built the parts: a decision statement, stories, criteria, a checked draft and a pinned-down metric. Tonight they go on one page, in the order a builder reads them, and the person who owns the decision signs it. Then the part most specs skip: a change arrives a month later, and the page has to say which changes need a new signature and which need only a line in the log. The capstone is Saffrondale's signed spec (a constructed example), scored on the checker, padded, changed and re-signed in front of you.

Part 1 · covers the one-page template

Everything on one page

5 min live

Each earlier session wrote one block of the spec. On the page they go in the order a builder needs them: why (the decision), for whom (stories), how to tell it works (criteria), exactly which number (the metric block), who agreed (the signature) and what has changed since (the log). One page is a constraint on purpose: what does not fit is usually what does not trace back to the decision.

LiveThe sections, in the order a builder reads them3 min▶

Title and version. "Saffrondale Clinics no-show dashboard v1.0". The version number matters the first time something changes.

Decision. The one sentence from session 1: when, who, the choice, the action.

Stories. From session 2, each with a role, a want and a reason that traces back to the decision.

Acceptance criteria. From session 3: Given, When, Then plus measurable bullets for freshness, speed and access.

The metric block. From session 5: Metric, Definition, Grain, Freshness, Source, Data owner.

Decision owner and Signed off. A name, a role, a date and the version signed.

Change log. One line per version: what changed, when, and whether it was re-signed.

While drafting, an "Open questions" list sits at the bottom, each with an owner (session 1's build-along). At handover it is empty, and it is deleted rather than left as a heading with nothing under it.

Self-studyWhy one page3 min▶

The limit forces cutting. A story that cannot be traced to the decision statement, a criterion nobody will check, a metric nobody asked for: on one page, each of those is taking space from something the builder needs.

The Agile Alliance glossary is blunt that a user story is not a document, and that its detail should grow as implementation nears. The one-page spec is consistent with both: it holds the detail agreed at handover, not every conversation that produced it. The conversations stay in the meeting notes; the page holds the outcome.

Some data products honestly need more than a page, for example a pipeline feeding several teams, or a model with a long list of exclusions. Then keep one page as the front sheet, with the decision, the owner and the signature, and link the longer sections from it.

Self-studyTemplates in your tools2 min▶

Issue trackers and wikis (Jira, Linear, Confluence, Azure DevOps and their peers) all offer page and ticket templates. Any of them can hold the seven blocks above. What they rarely hold by default is the decision statement at the top and a signature with a date at the bottom, so add both as fields. The tool matters less than the order and the signature.

Real world

What a standard says a good requirement is. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018, the international standard for requirements engineering, has a clause on the characteristics of individual requirements (5.2.5) and another on the characteristics of a set of requirements (5.2.6); those clause titles were read at source. The list itself is reported, because the standard is paywalled: necessary, appropriate, unambiguous, complete, singular, feasible, verifiable, correct and conforming. The separate clause for a set is the point for a one-page spec: requirements that are each fine can still fail together.

Part 2 · covers sign-off

Who signs, and what the signature means

5 min live

The decision owner signs: the person named as WHO in the decision statement. For the Constructed Saffrondale example that is Amara Osei, clinic operations lead (fictional). The signature is a promise in one direction, and the builder's check is a promise in the other.

LiveThe decision owner signs, the builder confirms3 min▶

What the owner's signature means: "If you build this as written, I will use it for the decision on line 2." It is not approval of the design, the tool or the SQL. It is a statement about the decision, and only the person who makes the decision can make it.

What the builder's confirmation means: "I have read every line and I have no open questions I can see." The builder runs the checker, reads the findings, and says so. If a builder signs off with questions in their head, those are the guessed requirements from session 1, now with a signature on top.

Who should not sign: the person who forwarded the ask, a committee, or the builder alone. A signature from someone who does not make the decision is a signature on a document, not on a decision.

LiveZero is a floor2 min▶

The checker reads wording and structure. The Quality User Story framework's semantic criteria, such as Conflict-free (no two stories contradict) and Problem-oriented (a story states the problem, not the solution), need a person; AQUSA's first version left them out on purpose because they require deep understanding of a requirement's content. A spec that says "Decision: whether to build a dashboard" passes the choice-word rule and is still a poor decision statement. Read the signed page once, aloud, with the owner, before anyone signs.

Self-studySign-off is not a contract2 min▶

Bill Wake's N in INVEST is Negotiable: a story "captures the essence, not the details", and details are worked out between the customer and the programmer. A signature does not end that. It fixes which version both sides agreed, so that the next negotiation starts from a known page instead of from two memories. Change control, Part 3, is how negotiation continues after the signature without dissolving it.

Real world

A published checker that knew its boundary. Lucassen, Dalpiaz, van der Werf and Brinkkemper designed AQUSA for "easily describable, algorithmically determinable defects", the clerical part of requirements engineering, and excluded the semantic criteria from its first version. Even inside that boundary it caught 93.8% of the defects it looked for, at 72.2% precision, on 1,023 real stories. A checker that clears a spec is a necessary step before signing, not a replacement for the person who signs.

Part 3 · covers change control

What forces a re-sign

5 min live

Every signed spec meets a change. The question is never whether to allow changes; it is which changes make the old signature untrue. A rule of thumb with two branches covers most of them, and the change log records which branch each change took.

LiveChanges that need a new signature3 min▶

A rule of thumb for these sessions, not a published standard: re-sign when the change could move a number the decision owner acts on, or change who sees it.

  • The decision. Monday becomes daily; reminder calls become text messages.
  • A definition. Late cancellations now count as no-shows (the Constructed Saffrondale v1.1 change below). Session 5 showed the same table giving 10.6% under one definition and 13.9% under the other.
  • Grain or freshness. Per clinic becomes per clinician; loaded by 06:00 becomes hourly.
  • Access. Receptionists can now see other clinics' patients.
LiveChanges that need only a log line2 min▶

Wording that changes no meaning, a layout change that moves no number, a typo, a new example that illustrates an existing rule. Each still gets a dated line in the change log, so the next reader can see that the page moved and why nobody re-signed. A silent edit to a signed page is how a signature quietly stops meaning anything.

Self-studyVersioning the spec, and what happens next3 min▶

Number the versions (v1.0, v1.1, v2.0), keep the old ones, and write each change log line as: version, date, what changed, why, re-signed or not. Wider project governance (milestones, risks, who is responsible for what) is the subject of Tech Project PMO, session 1; change control here covers only the spec.

Once signed, the spec is input to design. How builders turn requirements like "no older than 24 hours at 07:00" into architecture constraints is System Design, session 1.

Real world

Record the decisions, not only the result. The Data Cards work by Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson (2022) asks dataset documentation to explain the decisions that affect how a model performs, alongside sources and intended use. A spec's change log does the same job for a data product: a reader in six months should see not only that the no-show definition is different, but when it changed, why, and who agreed.

Build-along

Capstone: Saffrondale's signed spec, scored

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The Constructed Saffrondale example, signed at version 1.0, goes through four things on the checker: a score, a padding test, a real change and a re-sign. Then you write your own.

Score the signed spec. The checker below has loaded "6 · Signed spec". It prints 0 open builder questions for 259 words. Read the page top to bottom and find each block from Part 1.

Pad it, then unpad it. Press "Add more words". The checker prints 11 open questions for 327 words: 3 extra notes inside stories and 8 vague words. Press it again to take the padding off. A signed spec is the most tempting place to add reassuring sentences, and the worst.

★ Step 2 · what the padding adds to every story and checked criterionIt should be easy to use for all clinic staff. The numbers should always be accurate and up to date. Ideally it loads fast and works seamlessly on any device. Staff can then act on it as appropriate.

A change arrives (constructed). In April, Amara Osei asks for late cancellations, under 24 hours before the start, to count as no-shows. That changes a definition, so by Part 3's rule it is a new version and a re-sign. In the checker's text box, rewrite the Definition line if you like (it changes no checker count), then replace the last two lines with the two below and press "Check my text". The checker prints 1 open question: "No decision owner or sign-off", because the Signed off line has no date.

★ Step 3 · version 1.1, waiting for a signatureSigned off: pending re-sign of version 1.1 Change log: v1.0 2026-03-02 first signed version; v1.1 2026-04-06 late cancellations (under 24 hours) now count as no-shows

Re-sign. Replace the Signed off line with the line below and check again: 0 open questions.

★ Step 4 · version 1.1, re-signedSigned off: 2026-04-08 by Amara Osei, version 1.1

Find the checker's limit. Load "6 · Signed spec" again, change the Definition line to include late cancellations, and leave the old line "Signed off: 2026-03-02 by Amara Osei, version 1.0" in place. The checker still prints 0. It cannot know the signature is older than the definition. That is exactly why the change log and the re-sign rule exist: they carry what the rules cannot see.

★ Step 5 · a changed definition under an old signature (the checker prints 0)Definition: booked appointments with status "no-show", plus appointments cancelled under 24 hours before the start, divided by all booked appointments not cancelled 24 hours or more before the start time.

Write your own. Take the real ask from session 1's homework and write its one-page spec in the seven blocks from Part 1. Paste it into the checker and work it down to 0, then read it aloud to the decision owner before asking for a signature. Your count on the way down will differ from Saffrondale's; the end state should not.

★ Step 6 · the one-page skeleton<Product name> v0.1 Decision: <when>, <who> decides <which / whether / how many> <action>. As a <role>, I want <want>, so that <reason tied to the decision>. Acceptance criteria: Given <state> When <event> Then <observable outcome, with a number where one exists> Metric: <name> Definition: <numerator>, divided by <denominator>, <filters>, <time window> Grain: one row per <...> Freshness: <deadline with a number> Source: <system and table> Data owner: <role> Decision owner: <name, role> Signed off: <YYYY-MM-DD> by <name>, version <n> Change log: <version> <date> <what changed, why>

Put the change rule on the page before the first change. Agreeing at sign-off which changes need a re-sign costs one sentence. Arguing about it after the number has moved costs a meeting.

Homework

Your next spec

30 min
  1. Finish your own one-page spec from Step 6, at 0 open questions on the checker.
  2. Get it signed by the person who decides. Read it aloud with them first. Note every change they ask for; each is a question the checker could not see.
  3. Write the change rule into the page: one sentence naming which changes need a re-sign. Use Part 3's rule or your own.
  4. Audit one live data product you depend on. Can you find its decision, its metric definition and a dated signature? Write down the first missing block; that is the question its builder guessed.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Who should sign Saffrondale's spec, and what does the signature mean?

Only the person who makes the decision can say the spec serves it. The builder's confirmation is the other half: nothing left to guess.

2 · Which change to a signed no-show spec needs a new version and a re-sign?

A changes a definition, so the number on the screen moves: session 5's constructed week goes from 10.6% to 13.9%. The other three are log lines.

3 · The checker prints 0 for a spec whose definition changed under an old signature. What follows?

Like AQUSA, the checker stays inside what patterns can find. A person reads the meaning, and the change log makes the history visible.
