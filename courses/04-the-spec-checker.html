The bench: spec checker

Three sessions of advice become fourteen rules tonight, and the rules run for real in your browser. Part 0

Where we start

Sessions 1 to 3 gave Saffrondale's spec a decision statement, stories and testable criteria. Tonight a machine reads it. The checker does not judge whether the dashboard is a good idea; it counts what a builder would still have to ask. That number, open builder questions, is the headline on every rung. It is a count of findings, so it can go up as well as down, and the most useful thing it shows tonight is when and why it goes up.

Part 1 · covers what the checker reads and its 14 rules

Fourteen rules, each one a builder's question

9 min live

The checker first sorts every line into a kind: a field such as "Decision:" or "Grain:", a story, an acceptance criterion, or plain text. Then it runs the rules that apply to each kind, and finally the rules about the spec as a whole, which look for what is missing rather than what is wrong. Every finding becomes one open question.

LiveThe 14 rules, and where each one comes from5 min▶

Four rules are modelled on the Quality User Story framework (Lucassen, Dalpiaz, van der Werf and Brinkkemper, 2016). The other ten are teaching rules built for these sessions, and the widget labels each one "lab-only" on every finding.

Self-studyWhat the checker cannot see4 min▶

It reads wording and structure, never meaning. It cannot tell whether the decision is the right one, whether two stories contradict each other (QUS calls this Conflict-free), whether a story describes a solution instead of a problem (Problem-oriented), or whether the "so that" is a real reason or a reference to another story (Conceptually sound). Those are the semantic criteria, and they need a person.

It is also easy to satisfy on purpose. "Decision: whether to have a dashboard" contains a choice word and passes. "Then the page shows something" contains an observable verb and passes. A zero is a floor, not a verdict: it means a builder has no question the rules can detect, and the session 6 sign-off is where a person checks the rest.

And the rules are simple patterns, so they make mistakes in both directions. Part 2 measures one of them on real stories.

Real world

A published checker that chose the same boundary. AQUSA, the tool Lucassen and colleagues built on their framework, was designed for "easily describable, algorithmically determinable defects", which they call the clerical part of requirements engineering. Its first version implements the syntactic criteria and some pragmatic ones, and deliberately excludes the semantic criteria because those require deep understanding of a requirement's content. The checker on this page draws the same line.

Build-along · the bench

Six rungs, from one line to zero

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Click the six Saffrondale presets in order (Constructed Saffrondale example). Each is the spec as it stood after a session. Read the headline, then the findings: the line number tells you where, the bold name tells you which rule, the line under it is the question a builder would send you.

Rung 1, the one-line ask: 9 open questions. Eight findings are "Whole spec": no decision, no user, no criteria, no metric definition, no grain, no freshness, no source or owner, no sign-off. The ninth is on line 1, the vague word "maybe". Short text hides questions; it does not answer them.

Rung 2, the first draft: 19. The count more than doubles. The analyst wrote stories and criteria, and every one of them is now visible to the rules: three stories with no reason, one story with two asks ("a no-show dashboard and a prediction model"), six vague words (real-time, user-friendly, fast, easy, accurate, up to date) and three criteria with nothing to check. This is progress. Session 1's quiz asked why; this is the answer on screen.

Rung 3, stories fixed: 11. The decision statement from session 1 and the three stories from session 2 clear every story finding and the decision finding. What remains is the old criteria (three untestable, three vague words) and five data and sign-off questions.

Rung 4, criteria testable: 4. Session 3's Given, When, Then criteria and two measurable bullets clear six findings. One criterion, "Data on the page is no older than 24 hours at 07:00 each weekday", also answers the freshness question, so that one goes too. Left: the metric definition, the grain, the source and owner, the sign-off.

Rung 5, metric defined: 1. Session 5's block (Metric, Definition, Grain, Freshness, Source, Data owner) clears three findings. Only "No decision owner or sign-off" is left.

Rung 6, signed spec: 0. A named decision owner and a dated sign-off. Zero open questions: every rule passes, and a builder can start without guessing anything these rules can see.

★ Step 7 · plant a fault, and watch the checker catch itLoad "6 · Signed spec", then add this line under the receptionist criterion and press "Check my text": - The page loads fast. The checker prints 2 open questions: a vague word ("How fast is fast?") and a criterion with nothing to check. Now change it to "- The page loads in under 3 seconds." and check again: 0.

Run the checker on the spec you were handed, not only the one you wrote. A builder who pastes a received spec and sends back the finding list, line numbers and all, has turned "this spec is vague" into seven specific questions the requester can answer in one reply.

Part 2 · covers the rules on real stories

440 real stories, five base rates

5 min live

Saffrondale is constructed, so its numbers only show the rules working. Below the findings, the widget runs the five story rules over 440 real user stories: a seeded sample of 20 from each of 22 public project backlogs collected by Fabiano Dalpiaz at Utrecht University (Requirements data sets, 2018, CC BY 4.0). These are stories real teams wrote, unedited apart from the changes the widget lists.

LiveRead the two biggest bars honestly3 min▶

No reason given, 38.6%. Real teams leave out "so that" in well over a third of their stories. Is that a defect? It depends who you ask. The Quality User Story framework treats the ends as optional: a story should contain a role, a means and "(optimally) some ends". Mike Cohn writes "so that" in almost every story (62 of 64 in one backlog he counted) but does not consider it mandatory, because sometimes it adds no value. The rule here is lab-only and flags it anyway, because for a data product the reason is usually where the decision lives. Both positions are defensible; the widget says which one it takes.

Two asks in one, 27.7%. This rule is cruder than the published one. AQUSA's Atomic check also parses both sides of the "and" to confirm each half is a separate feature, and even so its own evaluation found Atomic precision of 51.18%: about half of its Atomic alarms were false. The checker here skips the parse, so it over-flags. One real story in the sample reads "As an Agency user, I want the header information box to show updated date AND time, so that I know when it was updated." That is one ask, and the rule fires on it.

Press "Check the next real story" a few times and judge each finding yourself. Agreeing with some and not others is the point.

Self-studyWhy the sample, and what changed in it2 min▶

The full public set has 1,680 non-empty story lines across 22 projects (the curator describes it as 22 data sets of 50 or more requirements each, gathered from web sources and sampled). The page ships a seeded sample of 20 per project so that every project counts equally and the file stays small. The licence, CC BY 4.0, asks us to say what we changed: curly quotes and dashes were straightened, other accents dropped, whitespace trimmed and empty lines removed. The widget prints the same list.

The curator notes that the set was gathered from public web sources, uses non-recent data and was originally used to study ambiguity detection. Treat the base rates as a description of these 440 stories, not of all user stories everywhere.

Real world

How good is a story checker, measured? Lucassen and colleagues ran AQUSA on 1,023 real user stories from 18 organisations and checked every alarm by hand. Overall it found 93.8% of the defects it looks for (recall) and 72.2% of its alarms were real (precision). They aimed for recall close to 100%, the bar Dan Berry argues an NLP tool for requirements needs to be useful, and did not reach it. A checker is a second reader with known blind spots, not a judge.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever

Add more words

5 min live

When a spec is challenged, the instinct is to add explanation. The "Add more words" button does exactly that: it appends one well-meaning sentence to every story and every checked criterion, the kind of sentence people really add ("It should be easy to use for all clinic staff."). Press it on any rung and compare the two numbers in the headline: words, and open questions.

LiveWhy padding raises the count, and where it does not3 min▶

On the signed spec, padding adds 11 findings: 3 "extra notes inside the story" (each story now carries a second sentence) and 8 vague words (easy twice, seamless twice, fast, accurate, up to date, as appropriate). Not one sentence answered a question that was open before, because none of them contains a number, a name or something to observe.

Look at what did not fire. The receptionist criterion already says "the 20 patients with the highest predicted no-show risk"; padding it with "accurate and up to date" adds no finding, because the line carries a number and those are quantity words. And the 24-hour freshness criterion keeps "fast" out of the count for the same reason, though "seamlessly" still fires. The honest reading: padding next to a measurable statement does less harm. Padding in place of one is all harm.

Self-studyThe words that pass, and why that is fair2 min▶

Six of the 19 vague words are quantity words: fast, quick, real-time, up to date, timely and accurate. Each one is exempt on a line that also carries a digit, on the reasoning that "fast: under 3 seconds" has answered its own question. The other thirteen (user-friendly, easy, simple, intuitive, efficient, flexible, robust, seamless, as appropriate, as needed, etc., and/or, maybe) always fire, because no number on the line tells you what "intuitive" means.

The rule is generous on purpose. A checker that fired on "fast" even next to "3 seconds" would teach people to ignore it.

Real world

Minimal is a published criterion, not a style preference. The Quality User Story framework's Minimal criterion says a story contains nothing more than a role, a means and ends; comments, expected behaviour and testing hints belong in separate notes. Its example from a real healthcare product appended "See: Mockup from Alice" and implementation notes to a story, and AQUSA reports all text after the first full stop, and anything in brackets, as not minimal. The padding button triggers the same check.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Check a real spec. Paste a spec, ticket or request you received this month into the checker. Write down the count and the three findings you most agree with.
  2. Find one false alarm. Among your findings or among ten real stories ("Check the next real story"), find a finding you think is wrong. Write one sentence on why the rule fired and what it missed.
  3. Pad, then cut. Press "Add more words" on rung 6, then edit the padded text until it is back to 0 without simply deleting the padding: replace each padded sentence with something measurable or move it to a notes line.
  4. Count the data questions. On rung 4, four questions are left. Write down what you would need to know to answer the first one, "Metric named but not defined", for a no-show rate. Session 5 starts there.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The signed spec scores 0 open questions. After "Add more words" it scores 11 with 68 more words. What is the lesson?

The checker never counts words. It counts findings, and the padding created them: 3 notes inside stories and 8 vague words. Padding next to a number (the 20-patient criterion) added nothing, which is the honest nuance.

2 · On 440 real stories, 38.6% trigger "Story gives no reason". How should you read that number?

It is a base rate for one rule on one public sample. Whether a missing reason is a defect is a real disagreement; the rule takes a side for data products, where the reason is usually where the decision lives, and says so.

3 · Why does the checker's "Two asks in one" rule fire on "show updated date AND time", and what does that tell you?

Simple patterns make mistakes in both directions. A finding is a prompt to look, and a person decides. That is also why a zero is a floor, not a verdict.
