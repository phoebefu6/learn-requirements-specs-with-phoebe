The data side: one name, one number

"No-show rate" sounds like one number. Hand the same table to two analysts and you can get two, or four, and every one of them is correct by its own reading. Part 1 · covers the metric definition

A metric definition has five parts

5 min live

A metric name is a label. A metric definition is a recipe: what you count, what you divide by, which records are in scope at all, over what stretch of time, and at what level of detail each row sits. Leave out any one of the five and the builder fills it in with a default. Here is the no-show rate from the constructed Saffrondale example, with every part named.

LiveNumerator, denominator, filters, time window, grain4 min▶

Numerator. What you count. For the constructed Saffrondale rate: booked appointments whose status is "no-show". Name the status value as it appears in the source, not a paraphrase of it.

Denominator. What you divide by. This is the part most often left implicit, and the part Part 2 shows splitting. Saffrondale's signed version (constructed): booked appointments not cancelled 24 hours or more before the start time. A slot cancelled a week ahead could be rebooked, so it never had the chance to become a no-show.

Filters. Which records are in scope at all: which clinics, which appointment types, whether test bookings or staff bookings are excluded. A filter you do not write down is a filter the builder will invent, or forget.

Time window. Which stretch of time one value of the rate covers. The decision is made each Monday about the coming week, so the window is the last full week, Monday to Sunday, by appointment date (not booking date: write that down too).

Grain. What one row stands for before anything is added up: one row per clinic per appointment date. The weekly rate is then a rollup of seven daily rows per clinic, and a monthly comparison is a rollup of the same rows. Pick the finest grain the decision needs, and coarser views come for free.

Self-studyOne format: the dbt Semantic Layer ratio metric4 min▶

Builders often write metric definitions down in code, and the requester's plain-language definition should map onto that code line by line. One widely used format is the dbt Semantic Layer. Its metrics overview lists the fields a metric carries: a name and a type (both required), plus a description, a label, a config and a filter. The types are simple, ratio, cumulative, derived and conversion.

A rate like the no-show rate is a ratio metric, and a ratio metric requires both a numerator and a denominator. The documentation draws a distinction worth stealing for any spec: a filter at the top level of the metric applies to both sides, while a filter inside the numerator or the denominator applies to that side only. "Exclude appointments cancelled 24 hours or more ahead" is a filter on the denominator side in Saffrondale's constructed definition; "all 6 clinics" is a top-level filter.

Grain works in two layers there too: the native time grain is set on the time dimension column, and the rollup grain (day, week, month) is chosen when someone queries the metric. That matches the advice above: specify the finest grain the decision needs in the spec, and let weekly and monthly views be rollups.

This is one way to write a metric down, not the way, and your team's tool may differ. Building a semantic layer is taught in Analytics Engineering, session b8; how one metric decomposes into a tree of drivers is in Data Literacy, session 4. The requester's job is the plain-language version; the builder's job is the code that matches it.

Real world

A metric format that refuses a half-defined ratio. In the dbt Semantic Layer (dbt Labs, Metrics overview), a metric of type ratio requires a numerator and a denominator before it is valid. The tool will not accept "no-show rate" without saying what is divided by what. A requirements spec has no such guard, which is why the checker's "Metric named but not defined" rule, a lab-only rule modelled on that ratio metric, asks the same question: a count of what, divided by a count of what?

Part 2 · covers "same name, two numbers"

Same name, two numbers

5 min live

Here is one clinic-week, constructed for this session with invented counts: 400 booked appointments. 40 were cancelled 24 hours or more before the start, 12 were cancelled less than 24 hours before, 38 were no-shows and 310 attended. Two analysts are asked for "the no-show rate". Neither makes a mistake. Watch where they split.

LiveWhere the numbers split4 min▶

Work the four readings through (all counts constructed):

ReadingNumeratorDenominatorRate
A · Analyst A38 no-shows400, all booked9.5%
B · Analyst B38 no-shows348, attended plus no-shows10.9%
C · The signed definition38 no-shows360, booked minus early cancellations10.6%
D · Late cancellations count as no-shows50, no-shows plus late cancellations36013.9%

Each split is a question a builder would otherwise guess:

Does a cancellation leave the denominator? Analyst A says no, every booking counts. Analyst B says every cancellation leaves. The signed definition says only cancellations made 24 hours or more ahead leave, because those slots could still be refilled.

Is a late cancellation a kind of no-show? For an operations lead whose slot sat empty, a cancellation an hour before the start feels exactly like a no-show. Reading D counts it that way. That is a legitimate choice, and it moves the number by more than three points.

What is the cut-off? "24 hours" is a choice too. Change it to 48 and the early and late piles change size.

None of these is a data question. They are business choices about what the decision needs, which is why the requester has to make them, in writing.

Self-studyPin it in the spec, not in the SQL3 min▶

Every one of the four readings ends up in a query somewhere. If the spec is silent, the definition lives only in that query, written by whichever builder got there first, and it is visible only to people who can read it. The next analyst asked for "the no-show rate" writes a second query, and the organisation now has two numbers with one name.

Writing the definition into the spec in plain language does three things. The requester can read it and say "yes, that is what I mean". Every builder implements the same recipe. And when someone quotes 13.9% in a meeting and someone else quotes 10.6%, there is a document that says which one is the agreed no-show rate, and what the other one is.

A practical test: read the definition aloud and ask a colleague to compute it by hand on a small set of made-up rows, as you will in the build-along. If they need to ask you anything, the definition is not finished.

If the organisation genuinely needs two readings (an operations view that counts late cancellations, and a reporting view that does not), give them two names. "No-show rate" and "empty-slot rate" can both exist; two "no-show rates" cannot.

Real world

Comparisons only mean something when the definitions match. A systematic review of 105 studies (Dantas, Fleck, Cyrino Oliveira and Hamacher, Health Policy, 2018) put the average no-show rate "of the order of 23%", from 43.0% in Africa to 13.2% in Oceania. The abstract does not say how each study defined a no-show, so we cannot tell how much of that spread is behaviour and how much is definition. That is exactly the reason to write yours down: if Saffrondale's constructed rate is ever set beside a published one, the first question will be "counted how?", and the spec should already answer it.

Part 3 · covers grain, freshness, source, owner and data cards

Grain, freshness, source, owner

5 min live

The definition says what the number is. Four more lines say where it comes from and when it can be trusted: one row per what, how old the data may be when someone acts on it, which table it is read from, and which person answers when it looks wrong. None of them should be chosen by looking at the database. All of them follow from the decision statement written in session 1.

LiveGrain and freshness, from the decision4 min▶

Start from the constructed decision statement: each Monday, the clinic operations lead decides which booked patients get a reminder call. Two data answers follow from that sentence alone.

Grain. The decision is weekly and per clinic, but the criteria from session 3 also ask for the same rate for each of the 4 weeks before, and a month-on-month comparison. A weekly grain would serve Monday and fight the monthly view (weeks do not divide into months). So the grain is daily rows per clinic: one row per clinic per appointment date. Weeks and months are rollups of those rows.

Freshness. The lead acts on Monday morning, before calls start, about last week. So last week's data, to the end of Sunday, has to be in place before Monday morning. The spec line is: loaded by 06:00 each weekday, with data to the end of the previous day. "Each weekday" because the receptionist's call list is used on other days too.

Notice what is not here: "real-time". Session 2 flagged it as a vague word in the first draft. A Monday decision does not need data from the last minute, and asking for it would cost a streaming pipeline nobody uses. Freshness is a number derived from the decision's cadence, not a wish.

One check: the session 3 criterion says data on the page is no older than 24 hours at 07:00 each weekday. A 06:00 load with data to midnight meets it with an hour of slack. The freshness line is the builder's promise; the criterion is how you test it.

LiveSource and owner3 min▶

Source. Name the table and how it arrives: the appointments table in the booking system, nightly extract (constructed). Not "the clinic data". There may be three tables with appointments in them (the booking system, the billing system, a spreadsheet a clinic keeps), and they will not agree. The spec picks one.

Data owner. Name the role who answers when the number looks wrong: for the constructed Saffrondale example, the head of patient access, who owns how appointment statuses are recorded. The builder of the dashboard is not the data owner. If a receptionist marks an arrived patient as "no-show" by mistake, the dashboard is correct and the number is wrong, and only the owner of the source can fix it.

The checker treats these as one question, "Which table does this come from, and who answers when it looks wrong?", and closes it only when both a Source line and a Data owner line are present. A source with no owner is a number nobody can correct.

Self-studyData cards for a model requirement4 min▶

The constructed Saffrondale ask still says "maybe a model": the receptionist story wants a list of next week's patients with a high no-show risk. A model adds a requirement a dashboard does not have: the data it learns from shapes what it predicts, and that has to be written down before training, not discovered after.

Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson (Data Cards, 2022) describe structured summaries of a dataset that cover its upstream sources, its collection and annotation methods, its intended use, and the decisions that affect how a model trained on it performs. Their abstract reports lessons from deploying over 20 Data Cards. The arXiv page says the paper was submitted to FAccT 2022; the published proceedings record is reported, not read.

For Saffrondale's constructed model requirement, the spec asks for a data card before any training starts, answering at least:

Which appointments? All 6 clinics, which appointment types, and the same no-show definition as the dashboard (or the model and the dashboard will disagree on what they measure).

Which period? The date range of the training data, and whether it includes any stretch when booking practice changed.

Which patients are excluded, and why? Every exclusion changes who the model can rank, so it is a decision for the requester, not a cleaning step for the builder.

Writing the requirements for an AI product in depth is taught in AI PM, session a3 and session b5.

Real world

Upstream sources, written down. The Data Cards work (Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson, arXiv:2204.01075, FAccT 2022 reported) treats the upstream sources of a dataset as a field to document, alongside how it was collected and what it is meant for, drawing on over 20 deployed Data Cards. The checker's "No data source or owner" rule is a lab-only rule built after that upstream-sources field: a number with no recorded origin cannot be checked, and a number nobody owns cannot be fixed.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Take one rate you report or receive (a churn rate, a conversion rate, a utilisation rate). Write its five parts: numerator, denominator, filters, time window, grain. Mark every part you had to guess.
  2. Find a second reading. For the same rate, write one other honest denominator someone could choose, and compute both on a small set of numbers you make up. How far apart are they?
  3. Derive freshness from the decision. For that rate, write the decision it serves and when it is made, then the freshness line that follows. Is the current refresh faster or slower than the decision needs?
  4. Name the data owner. Find out who answers when that number looks wrong at the source, not on the dashboard. If nobody does, write that down as an open question for session 6's sign-off.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In the constructed clinic-week, analyst A gets 9.5% and analyst B gets 10.9% from the same table. Which choice splits them?

Both use the same 38 no-shows and the same week, and both divisions are correct. The split is the denominator: whether cancellations leave it, and which ones. Only a written definition decides that.

2 · Why is the constructed Saffrondale freshness line "loaded by 06:00 each weekday" rather than "real-time"?

Freshness is derived from when someone acts on the data, not from what the pipeline could do. "Real-time" is a vague word in a spec, and for a Monday decision it buys cost without changing anything anyone does.

3 · The constructed Saffrondale spec still says "maybe a model". What does asking for a data card before training add to that requirement?

Data Cards (Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson, 2022) summarise upstream sources, collection methods, intended use and the decisions that affect model performance. In a spec, that turns hidden data-cleaning choices into visible requirements; it does not by itself make a model better.
