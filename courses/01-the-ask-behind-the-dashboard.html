The ask behind "a dashboard, and maybe a model"

Nobody wants a dashboard. They want to decide something on Monday morning with less guessing than last Monday. Tonight we find the decision hiding inside a one-line ask, count what it costs when a builder has to guess a requirement instead of asking, and write the one sentence every later part of a spec hangs from. The ask comes from Saffrondale Clinics, a constructed network of six outpatient clinics, and it is the kind of ask every analyst has received: "a no-show dashboard, and maybe a model".

Where we start

A spec is finished when the builder has no questions left. Part 1 · covers the decision a data product serves

Every data product serves a decision

5 min live

A dashboard, a metric, a model and a pipeline are all means. Each one exists so that somebody, at some moment, makes a choice with better information than they had before. When the ask names the means and not the choice, the builder has to reconstruct the choice from scraps, and two builders will reconstruct two different ones. Follow the chain from the ask to the outcome and notice where the builder actually learns what to build.

LiveDecision, actor, action: the three things an ask leaves out3 min▶

The decision is a choice between options: which patients get a call, whether to open a Saturday clinic, how many slots to overbook. If you cannot name the options, there is no decision yet, only a wish to look at numbers.

The actor is the person who makes that choice, and the moment they make it. "The clinic operations lead, every Monday at 08:00" tells a builder the grain (weekly), the freshness (Sunday night's data at the latest) and the audience (one person, not a wall screen) before a single metric is named.

The action is what physically happens next: calls placed, a rota changed, a referral sent. It decides the shape of the output. A person who will phone forty patients needs a sorted list with phone numbers, not a trend line.

None of the three is a technical question, and none of them needs the requester to know any SQL. They are the questions a requester can answer in two minutes and a builder cannot answer at all.

Self-studyThe job, not the feature (one-card recap)3 min▶

Why people ask for features rather than outcomes, and how to dig for the job behind the request, is a whole discipline of its own. It is taught in Product Thinking, session 1 and session 2, and the discovery conversation with a stakeholder is the subject of Asking the Right Questions as a Data Analyst.

These six sessions start one step later: the problem is clear enough to name, and the work is writing it down so that a builder who was not in the room can build it without guessing. If you find you cannot write the decision statement in the build-along below, that is the signal to go back to discovery, not to start building.

Self-studyFour data products, four decisions4 min▶

The same test works for every kind of data product a non-coder asks for:

The ask namesThe decision it might serve (constructed examples)What the builder learns
A dashboardWhich clinic gets the extra receptionist hours next monthMonthly grain, per clinic, compare clinics side by side
A metricWhether the reminder calls are worth the staff timeA before-and-after comparison, a defined window, a target
A modelWhich booked patients to call first this weekA ranked list, scored before the call is made, judged on the patients ranked highest
A pipelineWhether Monday's call list can be trusted at 08:00A freshness deadline and an owner to ring when it is late

Notice that "maybe a model" in Saffrondale's ask is only answerable once the decision is named. A model that ranks patients by no-show risk serves the "who gets a call" decision; it serves nothing if the decision turns out to be about rotas.

Real world

No-shows are a real decision problem, not a reporting curiosity. A systematic review of 105 studies across medical specialties (Dantas, Fleck, Cyrino Oliveira and Hamacher, Health Policy, 2018) put the average no-show rate at "of the order of 23%", highest in Africa (43.0%) and lowest in Oceania (13.2%). The determinants it found reported most often were a long lead time between booking and appointment, and a history of earlier no-shows. Both are known at booking time, which is what makes a "who gets a reminder call" decision possible at all; that inference is ours, not the review's.

Part 2 · covers the cost of a guessed requirement

Guessed, not asked

5 min live

Every open question in an ask gets answered by somebody. If the requester answers it in the spec, it costs one sentence. If the builder asks, it costs a message and a wait. If the builder guesses, it costs nothing today and a rebuild later, and nobody finds out which questions were guessed until the demo. That is why counting open questions matters: the guessed ones are invisible.

LiveWhy builders guess instead of asking3 min▶

Builders are not careless. They guess because asking has visible costs and guessing has none, at least this week. The requester is busy, the deadline is close, the question feels too small to bother anyone with, and the builder has a perfectly reasonable default in mind. "No-show" obviously means the patient did not arrive. Does a patient who cancelled an hour before count? One who arrived forty minutes late and was turned away? A telephone appointment nobody answered?

Each of those defaults is reasonable, and two reasonable builders will choose differently. The result is a dashboard that is correct by its own definition and wrong by the requester's, which is the most expensive kind of wrong, because nobody notices until the number is quoted in a meeting.

The fix is not "builders should ask more". It is to write the answers down before the work starts, which is what the next five sessions do, one kind of question at a time.

Self-studyAsked questions are not a failure of the spec3 min▶

A spec with zero open questions is the goal at handover, not on day one. The Agile Alliance glossary lists, among the common pitfalls with user stories, assuming that a story's level of detail stays fixed: detail should grow as the story nears implementation. The same glossary entry says plainly that a user story is not a document; it is a placeholder for a conversation.

So the honest picture is: early drafts are full of open questions, and that is healthy, because they are visible. The danger is a spec that looks finished, gets handed over, and still contains questions nobody wrote down. Session 4's checker makes this concrete: Saffrondale's one-line ask scores fewer open questions than its first written draft, because a checker can only count what is on the page.

Real world

"What is content?" When Lucassen, Dalpiaz, van der Werf and Brinkkemper built the Quality User Story framework (Requirements Engineering, 2016), they illustrated each defect with real stories from two Dutch software companies. Their example of an ambiguous story is "As a User, I am able to edit the content that I added to a person's profile page", and the defect they name is a single question: what is content? One word, and every builder who reads it either asks or picks a meaning.

Part 3 · covers the decision statement

One sentence the rest hangs from

5 min live

A decision statement is one sentence with four parts: when the decision is made, who makes it, the choice they make, and the action that follows. It goes at the top of the spec. Every story, criterion and metric that comes later should be traceable to it, and anything that cannot be traced to it is a candidate for cutting.

LiveThe four parts, and the test for each3 min▶

When. A cadence or a trigger: each Monday, at month end, when a referral arrives. Test: could the builder schedule a refresh from it?

Who. One named role, the person who will use the result and who can say whether the spec is right. Test: could you ask this person to sign the spec? Session 6 does exactly that.

The choice. Contains a choice word: which, whether, when, how many, how much, who. Test: can you list the options? "Which booked patients get a call" has options: every booked patient for next week. "Better visibility" has none.

The action. What happens in the world after the choice. Test: could you check, a month later, whether the action happened? That is where session 3's acceptance criteria and session 5's metric will come from.

Self-studyWhat a decision statement is not3 min▶

Not a goal. "Reduce no-shows by a fifth" is a goal. It is useful, and it belongs in the spec, but it does not tell the builder what anyone will do on Monday.

Not a metric. "No-show rate by clinic" is the thing the dashboard might show. Session 5 defines it properly; the decision statement says why anyone needs it.

Not a feature. "A dashboard with a filter by clinic" is a solution. The Quality User Story framework has a criterion for this in stories, Problem-oriented: a story specifies the problem, not the solution. The same discipline applies one level up.

Not several decisions at once. If the honest answer is "the ops lead decides who gets a call, and the finance lead decides the clinic budgets", write two statements. They will have different grains, different freshness and different owners, and probably two specs.

Real world

Documentation built around intended use. Google researchers Pushkarna, Zaldivar and Kjartansson (Data Cards, 2022) describe dataset documentation that records not only upstream sources and collection methods, but the intended use and the decisions that affect how a model performs. Their lessons come from deploying over 20 Data Cards. A decision statement plays the same role for a requested data product: it is the "intended use" field, written before anything is built.

Build-along

From one line to a decision statement

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Saffrondale's operations director sent the data team one line (Constructed Saffrondale example). You will run it through the spec checker from session 4, write the decision statement, check again, and leave with the remaining open questions sorted by the session that will close them. Everything runs in this page.

Run the ask through the checker. The widget below is the real rule engine from session 4. It has loaded "1 · The one-line ask". Read the headline and the nine findings. Each finding is written as the question a builder would have to ask. Notice that eight of the nine are about the spec as a whole ("Whole spec"): the ask is so short that most of what is wrong with it is what is missing.

★ Step 1 · the ask, exactly as sent (constructed)Can we get a no-show dashboard for the clinics, and maybe a model?

Name the actor and the moment. Who will open the result, and when? For Saffrondale, the operations director confirms it is the clinic operations lead, every Monday morning, before the reminder calls go out. Write it down in plain words before you worry about the sentence.

★ Step 2 · actor and momentWho: the clinic operations lead When: each Monday morning, before reminder calls start

Name the choice, with a choice word. What do they choose between? Not "look at no-shows": which booked patients get a reminder call this week. The choice word is "which", and the options are the booked patients for next week. If you find yourself writing "better visibility" or "insight", stop and ask what the person will do differently.

★ Step 3 · the choiceThe choice: which booked patients get a reminder call The options: every patient booked for next week, in all 6 clinics

Write the decision line and check again. Paste the two lines below into the checker's text box (replace what is there) and press "Check my text". The "No decision named" finding disappears: the line contains a choice word. Try the weaker version, "Decision: better visibility of no-shows", and watch the finding come back. Then answer the "maybe": Saffrondale decides the model question later, so delete ", and maybe a model" from the first line, and check once more.

★ Step 4 · the ask plus a decision statementCan we get a no-show dashboard for the clinics, and maybe a model? Decision: Each Monday, the clinic operations lead decides which booked patients get a reminder call.

The checker prints 8 open questions for the two lines above, and 7 once the "maybe" is gone. Your own wording may give a different count; what matters is that "No decision named" is no longer on the list.

Sort what is left. Copy the seven remaining findings into the spec as an "Open questions" list, each tagged with who must answer it. This is the honest state of Saffrondale's spec after session 1: the decision is clear, and seven questions are known and visible rather than waiting to be guessed.

★ Step 5 · Saffrondale's one-page spec, version 0.1 (constructed)Saffrondale Clinics no-show dashboard v0.1 Decision: Each Monday, the clinic operations lead decides which booked patients get a reminder call. Open questions (owner): 1. Who uses it, in which part of their job? (ops lead) - session 2 2. How will we both know it is done? (ops lead + builder) - session 3 3. No-show rate: a count of what, divided by what? (ops lead) - session 5 4. One row per what? (builder proposes, ops lead agrees) - session 5 5. How old may the data be on Monday morning? (ops lead) - session 5 6. Which table, and who answers when it looks wrong? (data team) - session 5 7. Who signs, and on which date? (ops director) - session 6

Read the open-question list aloud to the requester. It takes two minutes, and it is the fastest way to find out whether you have the right decision. If the requester says "no, it is really about the Saturday clinics", you have lost two minutes, not two weeks.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Find a real ask in your inbox for a dashboard, report, metric, model or pipeline, sent to you or by you. Write its decision statement in four parts: when, who, the choice, the action. If you cannot fill the choice, write down the question you would need to ask to fill it.
  2. Run it through the checker (session 4, or the widget above). Note the count, and how many of the findings are about the whole spec rather than a line.
  3. Find one guessed requirement in something you shipped or received: a definition, a filter, a refresh time that someone chose without asking. What did it cost when it surfaced?
  4. Name the users. For Saffrondale, list everyone who would open the no-show dashboard or act on its output. Session 2 turns that list into user stories.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is a decision statement a builder can work from?

B names when, who, the choice (with a choice word, "which") and the action. A is a wish with no choice, C is a solution, and D is a goal: useful in the spec, but it does not say what anyone will do on Monday.

2 · Why is a guessed requirement more expensive than an asked one, even though asking takes longer today?

Many guesses are reasonable, and asking does cost a message and a wait. The difference is visibility: an asked question is on the record the moment it is asked; a guessed one is found late, when changing it is most expensive.

3 · Saffrondale's one-line ask scores 9 open questions on the checker; the analyst's first written draft scores 19. What does that tell you?

The count that matters is the one at handover. Early drafts with many visible questions are healthy; session 4 walks the same spec from 19 down to 0, and also shows that padding a spec with words pushes the count back up.
