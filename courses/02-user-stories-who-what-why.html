Learn Requirements & Specs with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

User stories that say who, what and why

A decision statement tells the builder why the product exists. A user story tells them who will use one piece of it, what that person wants to do, and what changes for them when it works. Tonight we take the story format apart, check stories the way a reviewer and a machine would, split the ones that hide two asks, and look honestly at the argument over whether a dashboard needs stories at all. Then you rewrite five vague stories from the Constructed Saffrondale example until the checker finds nothing wrong with any of them.

Part 0

Where we start

Session 1 ended with one sentence at the top of Saffrondale's spec: "Each Monday, the clinic operations lead decides which booked patients get a reminder call." That sentence names one decision and one owner. But a no-show dashboard is opened by more than one person: receptionists, clinic managers, the operations lead herself, each wanting something slightly different. Stories are how those wants get written down one at a time, each small enough to build and check on its own. Everything tonight uses the Constructed Saffrondale example; no real clinic or patient is involved.

Part 1 · covers the story format and the 3 C's

Role, want, reason

5 min live

The standard story has three slots: as a (role), I want (something), so that (a reason). Each slot answers a question the builder would otherwise have to ask. The role says whose job this sits in and who to test it with. The want says what to build. The reason says what decision or action it serves, and that is the slot that should trace straight back to the decision statement from session 1. A story whose reason cannot be traced there is either serving a different decision or serving none.

LiveThe template and where it came from3 min▶

The template. "As a (role), I want (something), so that (benefit)." Mike Cohn, who did more than anyone to spread it, credits it to Rachel Davies and her team at Connextra in the UK in the early 2000s (Cohn, Advantages of the "As a user, I want" user story template, read at source). The Agile Alliance glossary dates the role-feature-benefit template to 2001.

The 3 C's. The same glossary dates the "3 C's" model to 2001: card, conversation, confirmation. The card is the short written story; the conversation is where the detail actually gets agreed; the confirmation is how both sides will know it is done, which is session 3's acceptance criteria. The glossary does not say who coined the 3 C's. Ron Jeffries is the usual attribution, and that is reported, not read at source.

Why it matters for a spec. The glossary puts it plainly: "a user story is not a document". A story is a placeholder for a conversation that still has to happen. So a story in a spec is a promise to talk, written down so nobody forgets who the talk is with and what it is for. A spec that is only a list of stories has not had the conversations yet.

Self-studyStories against tasks4 min▶

Bill Wake's 2003 article that gave us INVEST (Part 2) has a second half that is easy to miss: SMART tasks. A task is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-boxed (Wake, 2003, read at source).

The split is clean. A story is the who, what and why, written in the requester's language, and it belongs in the spec. Tasks are how the builder splits the work: "add the clinic filter", "write the weekly rollup", "schedule the Monday refresh". They belong in the builder's plan, whatever ticket tool the team uses, and the requester should never have to read them.

The Agile Alliance glossary lists a related pitfall: writing stories about screens, dialog boxes and buttons. "A dropdown with all six clinics" is a design choice, not a user story. The story is "as a clinic manager, I want my own clinic's weekly count"; whether that arrives as a dropdown, a link or an email is the builder's call, guided by the conversation.

Real world

"So that" is optional, and still almost always there. Mike Cohn writes that he does not consider the "so that" clause mandatory; sometimes, he says, it just does not add any value. Yet when he counted a recent backlog of his own, 62 of its 64 stories had one (Cohn, read at source). The person who argues hardest that the reason is optional still writes it almost every time, because it is usually the cheapest sentence in the story and the one a builder most often needs.

Part 2 · covers INVEST, QUS and splitting

INVEST, and the checks a machine can run

5 min live

A story in the right format can still be a bad story. Two checklists help. INVEST is a reviewer's checklist: six letters you run in your head while reading. The Quality User Story framework, QUS for short, is a researcher's checklist of 13 criteria, and some of them are simple enough for a machine to run. The most useful check of all is the one that spots two stories glued together with "and".

LiveINVEST, letter by letter4 min▶

Bill Wake published INVEST in 2003 (Wake, INVEST in Good Stories, and SMART Tasks, read at source). Six letters, each a question you ask of one story:

LetterThe questionA Saffrondale story that fails it (constructed)
IndependentCan it be built and shipped without waiting on another story?"A dashboard and a prediction model": the dashboard waits for the model
NegotiableIs the detail still open for the conversation, rather than fixed in advance?A story that dictates the chart type and the colours
ValuableDoes someone outside the build team get something from it?"Migrate the appointments table": a task, not a story
EstimableCould a builder give a rough size for it?"Predict who will not come", with no data source named
SmallIs it at most a few person-weeks of work?"The whole no-show reporting suite"
TestableCould you say, after building, whether it passed?"A user-friendly dashboard that loads fast"

Wake's own words for the last letter are short: "A good story is testable." That one letter is the bridge to session 3, where untestable wants become acceptance criteria with numbers in them.

One naming trap. Lucassen and colleagues, in the QUS paper below, expand the S as "Scalable". Wake's article says Small, and Small is the word to use: it is a size limit, and it is what makes splitting necessary.

LiveSplitting a compound story3 min▶

The QUS criterion Atomic says a story expresses exactly one feature. The tell is almost always a conjunction in the want: "and", "or", "&", "+". Saffrondale's first draft has the classic case: "As a manager, I want a no-show dashboard and a prediction model."

Glued together, the two halves fail three INVEST letters at once. They are not Independent (the dashboard waits for the model), not Small (a model is weeks of work on its own), and not Testable as one unit (no single test passes until both ship). Split, each half gets its own role, its own reason and its own test, as the figure shows. The dashboard story can ship in the first fortnight; the model story can wait until the data side is settled in session 5, and nobody is blocked in the meantime.

How to split. Cut on the conjunction. Then ask, for each half: who wants this one, and why? Often the two halves turn out to belong to different people. Here the dashboard serves the operations lead and the ranked list serves the receptionists, which was invisible while "a manager" wanted both.

An honest warning about the checker. The checker's "Two asks in one story" rule looks for " and " or " or " anywhere in the story. It does not parse both sides, the way the AQUSA tool does, so it over-flags: "ranked by risk and by clinic" is one feature with two sort keys, and it would still fire. Even AQUSA's more careful Atomic check had a precision of 51.18% in its evaluation (Lucassen et al., 2016). Treat the finding as a question to answer, not a verdict.

Self-studyThe 13 QUS criteria5 min▶

Lucassen, Dalpiaz, van der Werf and Brinkkemper (Improving agile requirements: the Quality User Story framework and tool, Requirements Engineering 21, 383-403, 2016) list 13 criteria in three groups:

GroupCriteriaWhat the group checks
SyntacticWell-formed, Atomic, MinimalThe shape of one story: role and means present, one feature, nothing extra
SemanticConceptually sound, Problem-oriented, Unambiguous, Conflict-freeWhat the words mean: the problem not the solution, no double meanings, no clashes
PragmaticFull sentence, Estimatable, Unique, Uniform, Independent, CompleteThe set of stories as a whole: no duplicates, one format, no gaps

Their tool, AQUSA, runs the syntactic criteria and some pragmatic ones, and deliberately leaves out the semantic group, because those "require deep understanding of requirements' content". That is the honest limit of any lint for stories: the defects that hurt most, like ambiguity, are the ones a machine struggles with.

What the session 4 checker runs. Three syntactic checks: Well-formed ("Story lacks a role or a want"), Atomic ("Two asks in one story", simpler than AQUSA's) and Minimal ("Extra notes inside the story": text in brackets or after a full stop, a spaced hyphen or an asterisk). Plus a vague-word list modelled on Unambiguous: words like fast, real-time, user-friendly, easy, efficient and "etc.", each paired with the question a builder would ask. The word list is the checker's own, not part of QUS. A sixth story rule, "Story gives no reason", is labelled lab-only, for reasons Part 3 explains.

Real world

The defects came from real backlogs. The QUS paper illustrates its criteria with stories taken from real Dutch software companies, not invented examples. Its Table 2 includes a story with no role at all (US1, a Well-formed failure), a story that is really two stories in one (US2, Atomic), and a story carrying the note "See: Mockup from Alice" inside it (US3, Minimal). Every one of those is in Saffrondale's five vague stories below, because every one of them turns up in ordinary backlogs.

Part 3 · covers the disagreement

Do data products need user stories at all?

5 min live

Practitioners disagree about this, and the disagreement is worth understanding rather than settling. A story template was built for software features used by many people. A dashboard often has one real user and one decision. Three camps write the same need three different ways, and each puts something different first: the person, the situation, or the decision.

LiveThree formats, one Saffrondale need4 min▶

The story camp. Mike Cohn's case for role-feature-benefit is that it keeps the who and the why on the card, where everyone sees them every time the story is discussed. Drop the role, and stories drift into "the system shall"; drop the reason, and nobody can tell which story to cut when time runs short.

The job-story camp. Paul Adams at Intercom described job stories on 28 June 2016 (How we accidentally invented Job Stories, Intercom blog, read at source). The format is "When ___, I want to ___, so I can ___". His critique of user stories is that they are formatted to describe functionality to be built, rather than the motivation behind it, and that a persona says less than the situation someone is in. The name "job stories" is reported to come from Alan Klement, who popularised the format.

Cohn's own position sits between them. He writes both, and thinks job stories are likely the better approach when the users' needs are not very distinct from one another (Cohn, Job Stories Offer a Viable Alternative to User Stories, read at source).

The plain problem statement camp. For a dashboard or a single metric, the user is often one person making one decision. Writing "as a clinic operations lead" on every card adds little when there is only one operations lead; the decision statement from session 1 may carry more than any story. The argument against: the moment a second person opens the dashboard, a statement written for one user hides the second user's needs.

Nobody has settled this, and there is no study that settles it for data products. The useful habit is to choose deliberately, per product, and to write down why.

Self-studyWhen the "so that" is optional4 min▶

Even inside the story camp, the reason clause is contested. The QUS framework treats the ends as optional: a story includes a role and a means, and optimally an end. Cohn does not consider "so that" mandatory either, though, as Part 1 showed, he writes it almost every time.

The session 4 checker takes a stricter line than either, and says so. Its "Story gives no reason" rule is labelled lab-only: it exists because a data product's reason is where the decision lives, and a missing reason is the most common way a story loses its link to the decision statement. On the 440 real stories sampled from the Dalpiaz corpus, it fires on 38.6% of them. That is not evidence that 38.6% of real stories are bad; it is evidence that many teams, reasonably, leave the reason out.

Practical rule for a data spec: keep the "so that" whenever it names a decision or an action, and treat a reason you cannot write as a signal to go back to session 1. If the reason is genuinely obvious, a requester and a builder can agree to drop it, and the checker's finding becomes a question you have already answered.

For where job stories come from (the "jobs to be done" thinking behind them), see Product Thinking, session 1; it is not retaught here.

Real world

Intercom wrote job stories because their personas were not helping. Paul Adams describes the format emerging inside Intercom's own product work, and the post's title is honest about how deliberate it was: they "accidentally invented" it. The team found that the situation and the motivation told them more about what to build than a persona did. Notice that this is one company's account of its own practice, written as a blog post, not a measured comparison; it is a strong argument, not a result.

Build-along

Rewrite five vague stories

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Here are five stories from the Constructed Saffrondale example first draft, the kind that turn up in any real backlog. You will run them through the session 4 checker, rewrite them in three passes (split, replace vague words, name a real role and reason), and check again. Each rewrite ends with a reason you can trace to the decision statement, or an honest note when it serves a different one. Everything runs in this page.

Check the five vague stories. The widget below is the real rule engine from session 4. Copy the five stories from the box, paste them into the widget's text box (replace what is there) and press "Check my text". It prints 20 open builder questions for this exact block. Thirteen are about individual stories: no reason given (four stories), two asks in one (one), role or want missing (one), extra notes inside a story (one), and six vague words: real-time, fast, user-friendly, etc., easy and efficient. The other seven are labelled "Whole spec": things no story can supply.

★ Step 1 · five vague stories (Constructed Saffrondale example)As a manager, I want a no-show dashboard and a prediction model. As a receptionist, I want to see no-shows in real time. As a clinic lead, I want a user-friendly dashboard that loads fast. I want the no-show numbers by clinic, etc. As a user, I want an easy way to see which patients might not come (and their phone numbers), so that the calls are efficient.

Split the compound story. Story 1 hides two asks behind "and". Cut it. The dashboard half goes to the person who actually decides about calls, the clinic operations lead, with a reason taken straight from the decision statement. The model half is really a list for the receptionists who make the calls; hold it until step 4, where it merges with story 5.

★ Step 2 · story 1, the dashboard halfAs a clinic operations lead, I want to see last week's no-show rate for each clinic, so that I can decide where reminder calls go.

Replace vague words with a number or a time. "In real time" becomes a time of day the receptionist can check: by 10:00. Ask the receptionist why, and the reason appears: freed slots can go to patients on the waiting list. Story 3 is harder. Asked what "user-friendly" and "fast" were for, the clinic lead says what they really want is to tell whether the calls are working. That is a different want, and a better story. If load time still matters, it becomes an acceptance criterion with a number in session 3, not a word in a story.

★ Step 3 · stories 2 and 3, rewrittenAs a receptionist, I want to see today's no-shows by 10:00, so that I can offer the freed slots to patients on the waiting list. As a clinic operations lead, I want to compare this month's no-show rate with last month's, so that I can tell whether the calls help.

Name a real role, give a reason, move the note out. Story 4 has no role: name the person who opens the weekly number, a clinic manager, and give them a reason. Story 5's "user" is a receptionist; "easy" and "efficient" become a ranked list and a concrete target, the top 20; and the bracketed phone numbers are part of the requirement, so they move into the want instead of sitting in a note. This is also where story 1's model half lands. Notice that rewrite 4's reason, planning reception cover, serves a staffing decision rather than the reminder-call decision. That is worth flagging to the requester: session 1 said two decisions may mean two specs.

★ Step 4 · stories 4 and 5, rewrittenAs a clinic manager, I want the weekly no-show count for my clinic, so that I can plan reception cover for the next week. As a receptionist, I want a list of next week's patients ranked by no-show risk, with phone numbers, so that I can call the top 20 first.

Check the five rewrites. Paste all five into the widget and press "Check my text". The widget prints 7 open questions, every one of them labelled "Whole spec": no decision line, no acceptance criteria, the metric not defined, no grain, no freshness, no data source or owner, no sign-off. Zero story-level findings. Those seven are not a failure of your stories; they are the work of sessions 1, 3, 5 and 6.

If you wrote your own rewrites instead, your count may differ. What matters is that the story-level findings are gone and every finding left says "Whole spec".

Read each reason aloud next to the decision statement. If the two sentences sound like they belong in the same meeting, the story fits the spec. If one sounds like a different meeting, as rewrite 4 does, you have found a second decision, and it deserves its own line in the open-questions list.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Rewrite three real stories from a backlog or a request you have seen. Give each a named role, one want and a reason you can trace to a decision. Run the before and after through the checker and note which findings disappeared.
  2. Find one compound story and split it on the conjunction. For each half, write who wants it and whether it could ship without the other. If the checker flagged an "and" that is really one feature, write one line saying why.
  3. Write one need three ways: as a user story, a job story and a decision statement. Which would your builder rather receive, and why? There is no right answer; write the reason down.
  4. Mark every untestable word left in your stories (fast, easy, accurate, up to date). Session 3 turns each one into an acceptance criterion with something to check.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these constructed Saffrondale stories fails INVEST's Testable letter, and why?

C has nothing to check: "user-friendly" and "fast" have no number, time or observable result, so it fails Testable, and "a dashboard" with no scope also strains Small. A, B and D each name something you can look at afterwards: a time, a weekly count, a list of 20 sorted by risk.

2 · What does the QUS criterion Atomic catch, and why does splitting help?

Atomic means exactly one feature. A is Well-formed, C is Minimal and D is the vague-word check modelled on Unambiguous. Splitting "a dashboard and a prediction model" lets the dashboard ship first and be tested on its own, instead of waiting for the model.

3 · A colleague asks: "Should our no-show dashboard spec use user stories or job stories?" Which answer is most accurate?

Nobody has settled this. Adams's post is one company's account, not a measured comparison; INVEST checks story quality, not the template; and plenty of data specs benefit from stories once more than one person uses the product. D presents it as what it is.
